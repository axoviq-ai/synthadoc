# ENIAC

ENIAC — the Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer — was the first general-purpose electronic computer, built at the University of Pennsylvania and publicly announced in 1946 after completion in late 1945.^[cs-milestones-overview.pptx:12-12]

## Construction and Specifications

Designed and built by [[john-presper-eckert|Eckert]] and [[john-william-mauchly|Mauchly]] at the University of Pennsylvania's Moore School of Electrical Engineering, ENIAC weighed approximately 30 tons and occupied around 1,800 square feet of floor space. It used over 17,000 vacuum tubes and performed roughly 5,000 additions per second — about 1,000 times faster than the electromechanical calculators it replaced. ENIAC was originally developed to compute artillery firing tables for the U.S. Army's Ballistic Research Laboratory.^[cs-milestones-overview.pptx:13-14]

## Programming by Patch Panel and Switches

Unlike modern stored-program computers, ENIAC was programmed by physically hand-wiring patch panels and setting thousands of switches to encode each computation. Setting up a new problem could take the programming team days or even weeks.^[cs-milestones-overview.pptx:15-15]

## The ENIAC Six

Six women — Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman (later Ruth Teitelbaum) — were responsible for the bulk of ENIAC's programming. Hired initially as human 'computers' to compute ballistic trajectories, they became the machine's primary operators and programmers, mastering its architecture in depth. Their contributions went largely unrecognised for decades, a pattern the broader history of computing has only recently begun to correct.^[cs-milestones-overview.pptx:15-16]

## Legacy

ENIAC demonstrated that electronic computation was practical at speeds far beyond electromechanical methods, ushering in the era of large-scale digital computing. Its limitations — particularly the difficulty of reprogramming — helped motivate the stored-program concept articulated in the [[von-neumann-architecture]] described in the 1945 EDVAC report.^[cs-milestones-overview.pptx:13-16]
