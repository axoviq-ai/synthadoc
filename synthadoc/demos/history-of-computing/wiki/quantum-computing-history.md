# Quantum Computing History

Quantum computing harnesses superposition, entanglement, and interference to solve problems that are intractable for classical computers. Unlike classical bits, qubits can exist in a superposition of 0 and 1 simultaneously, enabling a fundamentally different model of computation. The field emerged from theoretical physics and has progressed through decades of conceptual breakthroughs, algorithmic discoveries, and recent hardware demonstrations.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:5-5]

## Feynman's Proposal (1981)

[[richard-feynman]] proposed at a 1981 conference that quantum systems could only be efficiently simulated by quantum computers. His insight — that nature is fundamentally quantum mechanical and that classical computers struggle to simulate it — laid the conceptual foundation for the entire field.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:9-11]

## Shor's Algorithm (1994)

Peter Shor demonstrated that a quantum computer could factor large integers in polynomial time, threatening RSA encryption and galvanising government and industry investment worldwide. The algorithm showed a concrete, practical problem where quantum computers offered an exponential speedup over the best known classical methods, transforming quantum computing from a theoretical curiosity into a strategic research priority.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:13-15]

## Grover's Algorithm (1996)

Lov Grover showed that quantum search could find an item in an unsorted database of N items in O(√N) steps versus O(N) classically. While the speedup is quadratic rather than exponential, the algorithm has broad applicability to combinatorial search and optimisation problems.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:17-19]

## Google Quantum Supremacy (2019)

Google's Sycamore processor completed a specific sampling calculation in 200 seconds that classical supercomputers could not reproduce in reasonable time. This demonstration marked the first claimed instance of quantum supremacy — a term describing tasks where quantum hardware outperforms any classical machine.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:21-23]

## Logical Qubit Demonstrations (2023)

IBM, Google, and IonQ demonstrated error-corrected logical qubits, a prerequisite for fault-tolerant quantum computation at scale. Physical qubits are highly susceptible to noise; encoding information across multiple physical qubits to form a single logical qubit is a critical step toward building reliable, large-scale quantum machines.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:25-27]

## Relationship to Classical Computing

Quantum computing does not replace classical computing. It solves a specific class of problems — optimisation, simulation, cryptography — exponentially faster. The field remains pre-commercial but attracts billions in investment from governments and technology companies worldwide.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:29-29]

## Key Reference

The foundational textbook for the field is Nielsen & Chuang, *Quantum Computation and Quantum Information* (2000), which remains the standard reference for students and researchers entering quantum computing from [[artificial-intelligence-history]], physics, or computer science backgrounds.^[quantum-computing-primer.png:31-31]
