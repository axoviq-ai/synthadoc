# Microprocessor

The microprocessor — a complete central processing unit fabricated on a single silicon chip — is the device that made the [[personal-computer-revolution]] physically possible. Before the microprocessor, a CPU required a cabinet full of discrete components; after it, a processor could fit on a fingernail-sized piece of silicon and cost a few dollars.

## Origins at Intel (1971)

The Intel 4004, released in 1971, was the first commercially available microprocessor — a complete CPU on a single chip. It was designed by Federico Faggin, Ted Hoff, and Stanley Mazor at Intel for a line of calculators being developed by the Japanese company Busicom. Intel purchased the rights to sell the 4004 for non-calculator applications, recognising it as a general-purpose computing engine rather than a specialised controller.^[ceruzzi-history-modern-computing.txt:19-19]

The 4004 contained 2,300 transistors (a milestone made possible by [[transistor-and-microchip|integrated circuit]] technology pioneered by Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments and Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor), operated at 740 kHz, and processed data in 4-bit chunks.^[ceruzzi-history-modern-computing.txt:21-21]

## The 8080 and the Altair

The 4004 was followed by the Intel 8008 in 1972 and the Intel 8080 in 1974, each substantially increasing transistor count and performance. The 8080 became the processor at the heart of the MITS Altair 8800 in 1975 — the symbolic start of the [[personal-computer-revolution]] — and later powered the CP/M operating system and a generation of S-100 bus machines.^[ceruzzi-history-modern-computing.txt:21-25]

The 8086, introduced in 1978, established the 16-bit architecture whose descendants (the x86 family) still dominate server and desktop computing.^[ceruzzi-history-modern-computing.txt:21-21]

## Moore's Law

Gordon Moore, co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and later Intel, observed in a 1965 paper that the number of components per integrated circuit had been doubling roughly every year and predicted this would continue for at least a decade. The observation was later revised to a doubling approximately every two years and became known as Moore's Law. Held as an empirical trend for over fifty years, it drove the relentless reductions in cost and size that made both the microprocessor and the personal computer economically feasible.^[ceruzzi-history-modern-computing.txt:15-15]

## Microprocessors and the PC Industry

The decision by IBM in 1981 to build its Personal Computer from commercially available components — including the Intel 8088 processor — and to publish the full technical specifications created an open hardware architecture. Combined with Microsoft's MS-DOS, this established the Wintel platform that became the industry standard, with the x86 instruction set surviving into modern 64-bit processors.^[ceruzzi-history-modern-computing.txt:37-39]
